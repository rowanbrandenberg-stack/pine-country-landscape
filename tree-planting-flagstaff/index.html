Why plants fail in Flagstaff

Most plants that die here were chosen or planted for somewhere else. At nearly 7,000 feet you are dealing with a short growing season, late frosts, drying wind, heavy snow load, alkaline volcanic cinder soil and elk that will browse anything tender. Rowan learned the trade in a Flagstaff nursery yard, and every plan starts with what actually thrives here.

What we plant

  • Quaking aspen, Colorado blue spruce, ponderosa and pinyon pine
  • Native and xeric shrubs, grasses and perennials
  • Native berms with boulders and drip
  • Rock, cinder and mulch beds
  • Large tree transplants set with machinery

Planted right, then watered right

Every tree gets a properly sized hole, amended backfill where it helps, a watering basin and, where it makes sense, drip on a timer. We give you a simple establishment watering schedule, and we offer winter watering visits so new trees do not dry out over our dry, windy winters.

Elk and deer

If you are near open forest, we plan for browse pressure from day one: species choice, caging for young trees, and placement that keeps tender plants close to the house.

Questions

Tree planting and design, answered.

What trees grow best in Flagstaff?

Ponderosa pine, pinyon, Colorado blue spruce, quaking aspen in the right spot, and a handful of hardy ornamentals. Species choice depends on your sun, water and elk pressure, which is what the consultation is for.

When is the best time to plant trees in Flagstaff?

Spring after the last hard frost and early fall are best. Fall planting gives roots time to establish before summer heat.

How much does a design consultation cost?

$150 for a 90 minute on site visit, and the fee is credited toward your project if you move forward.

Do you water new plantings?

Yes. We set up drip where it fits and offer winter watering visits for new trees and shrubs.

Can you move or transplant large trees?

Yes. We set large balled trees with machinery when access allows.
